\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Single Cell Analysis}

Single cell sequencing technologies have enabled increasingly detailed characterization of cellular heterogeneity \citep{tangMRNASeqWholetranscriptomeAnalysis2009, ginhouxSinglecellImmunologyPresent2022}. Assays targeting biomolecules such as RNA \citep{buettnerComputationalAnalysisCelltocell2015}, chromatin \citep{lakeIntegrativeSinglecellAnalysis2018}, and immunoreceptors \citep{wangIntegratingSinglecellRNA2025} generate high-dimensional feature spaces that describe cell states at multiple regulatory layers. These data have, in turn, spurred the development of computational methods to analyze the statistical structure and distributional properties of cell populations across diverse experimental conditions \citep{lueckenCurrentBestPractices2019, conesaSurveyBestPractices2016, heumosBestPracticesSinglecell2023, loveModeratedEstimationFold2014}.

Single cell analysis has revealed that cell states tend to lie near low-dimensional manifolds embedded in high-dimensional feature space \citep{moonManifoldLearningbasedMethods2018, mcinnesUMAPUniformManifold2018}. Roughly speaking, a manifold is a topological space that locally resembles Euclidean space, while potentially exhibiting global curvature or more complex structure \citep{tuIntroductionManifolds2011}. In the context of cellular data, this implies that interpolation between nearby cells in feature space is more likely to produce biologically plausible states, but that interpolation between distant cells may yield implausible ones. This apparent manifold structure is often interpreted as reflecting underlying nonlinear regulatory relationships governing cell state \citep{huguetManifoldInterpolatingOptimalTransport2022}. This perspective is closely related to Waddington's epigenetic landscape, which conceptualizes development as trajectories of cells rolling down a landscape shaped by gene regulation and epigenetic factors \citep{waddingtonStrategyGenes2014}.

The fact that only a small region of feature space is biologically feasible has led to the development of computational analysis tools that conform to this structure. Graph-based methods exploit cell neighborhood heuristics to fit approximate manifolds \citep{moonVisualizingStructureTransitions2019, dijkMAGICDiffusionbasedImputation2017, dijkRecoveringGeneInteractions2018}. More recently, deep learning methods such as variational autoencoders \citep{lopezDeepGenerativeModeling2018, xuProbabilisticHarmonizationAnnotation2021} and denoising diffusion \citep{luoScDiffusionConditionalGeneration2024} models have enabled translating between arbitrary distributions, allowing smooth interpolation between distributions on the single cell manifold \citep{lotfollahiScGenPredictsSinglecell2019, kleinCellFlowEnablesGenerative2025}.

\section{Trajectory inference}

A fundamental limitation of single cell sequencing is its destructive nature: a cell can only be sequenced at a single timepoint, ruling out traditional longitudinal analysis methods. This limitation has catalyzed a plethora of computational analysis approaches to infer trajectories underlying cell development in high-dimensional feature space \citep{saelensComparisonSinglecellTrajectory2019}. In order to study dynamic biological processes like differentiation or perturbation response kinetics, it is necessary to sample cells at different phases of the dynamic response. A single timepoint may suffice for some pseudo-equilibrium processes, for example the cell cycle in homeostasic cells \citep{stallaertStructureHumanCell2022} or asynchronous differentiation in hematopoietic stem cells \citep{macaulaySingleCellRNASequencingReveals2016, weinrebFundamentalLimitsDynamic2018}; however in many cases it is necessary to sequence independent samples across timepoints \citep{schiebingerOptimalTransportAnalysisSingleCell2019, kirschenbaumTimeresolvedSinglecellTranscriptomics2023}.

The challenge of reconstructing cell dynamics from snapshot data has driven the development of computational frameworks to reveal how cells traverse the cell state manifold. We can divide these into three broad classes, comprising pseudotime, velocity, and time-resolved methods.

\subsection{Pseudotime methods}
Pseudotime methods seek to assess the relative degree of differentiation of cells from an initial state, which is generally specified a priori. These methods often make use of manifold approximations via a $k$-nearest neighbors ($k$-NN) graph, with prominent methods like Monocle \citep{trapnellDynamicsRegulatorsCell2014} and Slingshot \citep{streetSlingshotCellLineage2018} utilizing minimum spanning trees to define trajectories along the cell state manifold, assigning pseudotime based on the graph distance from an initial state. This approach is generalized by methods like diffusion pseudotime \citep{haghverdiDiffusionPseudotimeRobustly2016}, which assigns a pseudotime value by applying a heat kernel over the single cell $k$-NN graph.

Pseudotime methods excel at ordering cell states along acyclic trajectories \citep{weinrebLineageTracingTranscriptional2020}, and even some cyclic trajectories \citep{stallaertStructureHumanCell2022}. However, in differentiation processes with large jumps in cell state space, pseudotime may incorrectly infer cell orderings \citep{hudsonTechnologyMeetsTILs2022}.

\subsection{Velocity methods}
Velocity methods exploit molecular mismatches between experimentally measured modalities (e.g., between spliced and unspliced mRNA data, or between chromatin accessibility and downstream gene expression) to fit an instantaneous cell velocity in feature space using idealized conservation laws \citep{lamannoRNAVelocitySingle2018, bergenGeneralizingRNAVelocity2020, chenDeepVeloSinglecellTranscriptomic2022}. While originally designed to deal with conversion rates between spliced and unspliced mRNA \citep{lamannoRNAVelocitySingle2018}, recent works have extended this approach to multi-omic data by modeling the upstream effects of chromatin accessibility on transcription \citep{liMultiomicSinglecellVelocity2023}.

Velocity methods are inherently noisy, since they both require approximations of complex regulatory cascades and assess instantaneous rates of change for stochastic cellular processes \citep{gorinRNAVelocityUnraveled2022}. As such, they may be unreliable in isolation, and have been found to work best when used in conjunction with other frameworks such as $k$-NN based manifold learning or multi-modal integration \citep{ranekIntegratingTemporalSinglecell2022, langeCellRankDirectedSinglecell2022}.

\subsection{Time-resolved methods}
Time-resolved methods seek to reconstruct continuous cell trajectories using discretely sampled timepoints, explicitly incorporating temporal information. One prominent class of time-resolved inference methods uses optimal transport (OT) to fit cell-state couplings by minimizing the cost of transporting cell distributions between timepoints \citep{schiebingerOptimalTransportAnalysisSingleCell2019, kleinMappingCellsTime2025}. This is especially appealing for non-equilibrium systems that evolve over time, such as immune responses to acute infection, or cell responses to transient stimulation. While time-resolved OT methods are more robust to transient noise than velocity and pseudotime methods, their reliability degrades for large gaps between timepoints. Therefore, OT methods are most suitable for datasets with fine temporal resolution.

\subsection{Theoretical foundations}
All of the approaches above share a unifying theoretical framework: the representation of cell trajectories as a diffusion-drift process along the cell-state manifold \citep{lavenantMathematicalTheoryTrajectory2021, yeoGenerativeModelingSinglecell2021, weinrebFundamentalLimitsDynamic2018}. This allows changes in cell state to be conceptualized as arising from deterministic drift effects (e.g., gene regulatory networks) alongside stochastic diffusion. Under this framework, methods such as diffusion pseudotime represent random walks over the cell state manifold, while entropic OT becomes a Schr\"odinger bridge encoding the lowest-energy coupling between two distributions under Brownian motion \citep{parisetUnbalancedDiffusionSchrodinger2023, tongSimulationfreeSchrodingerBridges2024}. The key goal of many trajectory inference methods is thus to quantify single cell drift effects, thereby revealing the regulatory relationships underlying cellular trajectories \citep{shaReconstructingGrowthDynamic2024}.

This theoretical framework has increasingly informed trajectory inference methods, and many modern approaches integrate some combination of instantaneous velocity, manifold structure, and temporal regularization into a single method. For example, TrajectoryNet \citep{tongTrajectoryNetDynamicOptimal2020} utilizes a manifold distance along the $k$-NN graph to solve a dynamic OT problem, while CurlyFM \citep{petrovicCurlyFlowMatching2025a} regularize trajectory streamlines using instantaneous velocity estimates.

\section{CD8 T cells}

CD8 T cells are crucial mediators of cell immunity and part of the adaptive immune system. A hallmark feature of CD8 T cells is cytotoxic potential, the ability to directly target and kill infected or malignant cells \citep{chungArchitecturalDesignCD82021, raskovCytotoxicCD8Cells2021}. As members of the adaptive immune system, these cells exhibit high specificity for pathogen-derived epitopes, enabling potent immune responses and forming the basis for long-lived immunological memory \citep{altan-bonnet916SystemsImmunology2012, kaechEffectorMemoryTcell2002}.

\subsection{Infection response dynamics}
As CD8 T cells are crucial mediators of immunoserveillance in infection and cancer \citep{steinertQuantifyingMemoryCD82015, marelli-bergHighwayCodeCell2008}, understanding the principles and signals governing their function and differentiation is important in the context of vaccinology and immunotherapies. Naive CD8 T cells become activated after encountering cognate antigen presented on major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) on infected or antigen-presenting immune cells. The cognate antigen is bound by a specific T-cell receptor (TCR), leading to a cell signaling cascade that activates proliferative and effector programs in the T cell \citep{jrImmunobiology2001}. This signaling leads to a wave of proliferative clonal expansion, where the population of activated CD8 T cells can increase up to 500,000-fold \citep{zhangCD8CellsFoot2011} in order to execute an immune response. Naive T cells patrol the circulatory and lymphatic systems to survey for the presence of antigen, and upon activation in draining lymph nodes, are capable of infiltrating infected tissues \citep{marelli-bergHighwayCodeCell2008}.

Once antigen is eradicated, the cessation of inflammatory proliferation and survival signals leads to the contraction of terminally differentiated T cell populations \citep{dcruzSurvivingCrashTransitioning2009}, leaving a remnant group of stem-like long-lived memory T cells \citep{goldrathMolecularProgramInduced2004, luckeyMemoryMemoryCells2006}. These memory cells protect against re-infection, and are crucial in the success of vaccines \citep{wherryMemoryCD8TCell2004, kaechEffectorMemoryTcell2002}.

Heterogeneity in the early effector T cell population leads to a spectrum of differentiation states, encompassing differential cytotoxic, proliferative, and migratory capacities \citep{bestTranscriptionalInsightsCD82013, sarkarFunctionalGenomicProfiling2008, milnerHeterogenousPopulationsTissueResident2020, kaechHeterogeneityCellfateDecisions2007}. The sources of this heterogeneity are multifaceted, and include clonal variation in antigen binding affinity, extracellular signals received during activation, and even innate biases in the naive CD8 T cell pool \citep{yoonCellCycleTime2010, martinetTypeInterferonsRegulate2015, wherryViralPersistenceAlters2003, kaechTranscriptionalControlEffector2012}. Understanding how these factors work together to influence CD8 T cell states and memory is crucial for engineering durable immune responses \citep{ellisGeneticEngineeringCells2021, kaechEffectorMemoryTcell2002}.

\subsection{CD8 T cell subsets during acute infection}

Although CD8 differentiation comprises a continuum of cell states, there are several discrete subsets that are defined by key features and functions during the acute infection response. Naive CD8 T cells express CD62L (\textit{Sell}), which helps them patrol lymph nodes where antigen priming and T cell activation occurs. These are the most stem-like subset, having not yet encountered their cognate antigen. Activated CD8 T cells that have encountered antigen initiate gene expression programs that prepare them for effector responses, but are not yet terminally fated. These "poised" cells are distinguished from naive cells by downregulation of CD62L and expression of activation markers like CD44 (\textit{Cd44}) and CD25 (\textit{Il2ra}), the latter of which receives signals from IL-2 which are vital for proliferation and survival. Activated cells develop into early effectors (T\textsc{ee}), which give rise to distinct terminal effector (T\textsc{te}) and memory precursor (T\textsc{mp}) populations. T\textsc{te} are distinguished by high expression of KLRG1 (\textit{Klrg1}) and low expression of CD127 (\textit{Il7r}). These cells are cytotoxic and terminally differentiated. In contrast, T\textsc{mp} are defined by high CD127 and low KLRG1 expression, and are more quiescent and stem-like than their more differentiated counterparts \citep{yuzefpolskiyEarlyCD8Tcell2015, kaliaProlongedInterleukin2RaExpression2010, joshiInflammationDirectsMemory2007}.

After infection is cleared, remaining antigen-specific CD8 T cells are typically referred to as bona fide memory cells, and may be further subdivided into central memory (T\textsc{cm}), effector memory (T\textsc{em}), and tissue-resident memory (T\textsc{rm}) states. T\textsc{cm} primarily reside in the lymphatic system, and express high levels of CD62L. These are the most stem-like memory cells, and have the highest capacity to generate durable responses upon re-exposure to antigen. T\textsc{em} do not express CD62L, and patrol the circulatory system as well as transiently entering peripheral tissues. Finally, T\textsc{rm} permanently reside in peripheral tissues, expressing high levels of CD69 (\textit{Cd69}) and often CD103 (\textit{Itgae}), which facilitate tissue homing and retention \citep{jamesonNamingMemoryTCell2021, jamesonUnderstandingSubsetDiversity2018, muellerMemoryCellSubsets2013, parkEmergingRoleResident2015}. All these populations may be further subdivided based on differentiation status. For example, the most terminally differentiated portion of T\textsc{em} may be referred to as terminal effector memory (T\textsc{tem}) \citep{milnerDelineationMolecularlyDistinct2020}; similarly, T\textsc{rm} can be divided into progenitor (T\textsc{rm}-prog) and terminal (T\textsc{rm}-term) subtypes, depending on gene expression clustering as well as spatial location \citep{kurdEarlyPrecursorsMolecular2020, reina-camposTissueresidentMemoryCD82025}.

The origin of memory CD8 T cells is still a topic of debate in the field. One traditional view is that memory cells "de-differentiate" from effector populations \citep{youngbloodEffectorCD8Cells2017, zhouDifferentiationPersistenceMemory2010}, while another is that memory cells differentiate directly from memory-poised naive populations through the intermediate T\textsc{mp} population \citep{ferreiraCentralMemoryCD82020}. It appears likely that both processes may take place to varying degrees, as evidenced by recent lineage-tracing studies \citep{moldDivergentClonalDifferentiation2021, herndler-brandstetterKLRG1EffectorCD82018, abdullahHierarchalSinglecellLineage2024}. Simulations suggest that multiple pathways for memory formation may have a functional role in buffering the T cell response against variations in viral replication rate \citep{abadieReversibleTunableEpigenetic2024}. There is also evidence that circulating precursors of T\textsc{rm} populations may exhibit early fate biases \citep{kokCommittedTissueresidentMemory2020}, though the degree to which T\textsc{rm} differentiation arises from predetermined precursors versus effector de-differentiation remains unclear.

\section{Motivation and Objectives}

Although experimental strategies can define memory T cell differentiation paths and the signals that control their fates, computational approaches offer unique potential to address aspects of T cell biology that are experimentally inaccessible. Here, we develop novel computational methods and apply them to resolve CD8 T cell differentiation pathways during infection. The findings in this dissertation clarify the complex trajectories of early-differentiating T cells in this context.

In Chapter 2, we introduce TKME (trajectory kernel mean embedding), a computational approach for "featurizing" OT trajectories. TKME creates compact summaries of cell states across entire experimental time courses, enabling analysis tasks such as trajectory clustering and statistical tests for differential trajectory enrichment under experimental perturbations.

In chapter 3, we fit OT models to the CD8 T cell responses during acute infection, showing that OT recovers clonal expansion, contraction, and differentiation dynamics. TKME facilitates trajectory comparisons between biological compartments by integrating cells from disjoint timepoints into a shared trajectory space. Multi-compartment OT modeling further recapitulates early T cell migration dynamics. We use this framework to predict phenotypes associated with differential arrival times in the small intestine intraepithelial lymphocyte (siIEL) compartment and to identify transcriptional signatures of early T\textsc{rm}-fated cells in circulating precursor populations. These analyses reveal the interplay between CD8 T cell differentiation and migration, nominating transcription factors with context-dependent roles in fate specification.

This dissertation advances OT-based single-cell trajectory analysis in two ways: (1) by introducing a featurization framework for high-dimensional trajectories, and (2) by extending OT inference to multi-compartment settings. Each advance addresses a key gap in single-cell analysis, with experimental validation of biological insights where appropriate.
